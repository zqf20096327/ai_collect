- **[Procedure without Judgment: What Small Language Models Learn under an Exact Verifier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21529)**  `arXiv:2611.21529`  
  _Arunkumar V, Manoranjan Gandhudi, Gangadharan G. R., Arun Prakash, S. Senthilkumar_
  <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
  When an authorization policy blocks an agent's tool call, the audit log should name the facts that forced the decision and nothing more. For an executable policy over an enumerable context space this minimal sufficient reason can be computed exactly, so an explanation is scored against ground truth rather than a proxy for it. We use that exactness to ask what a model can learn from a verifier that has no error in it. The explainer sees one request and one decision, never the policy, and has thirty turns in which it may call the policy on altered requests and ask whether a candidate set of facts is sufficient. Frontier models find the exact reason about half the time with the tools alone, and one sentence telling them to shrink the answer lifts Haiku 4.5 from 58% to 75%. A 4B model fine-tuned on 2000 traces of the deletion algorithm reaches 92% on the policy it was trained on. Teacher kind is what moves this: at matched data size the algorithm-taught student leads the frontier-taught one in all three training seeds, and the two arms never overlap. What the student learns is a fixed schedule of sufficiency checks, and the schedule does not travel. On three public policies it never saw, its answers stay sufficient (94%) and stop being minimal (48%), while Haiku under the same sentence is exact on all 47 episodes. Reinforcement learning from the exact reward leaves held-out accuracy where it was and inverts the strategy, from probing the policy to spending the entire budget on sufficiency checks. The oracle made the effect of training legible, but it did not make training better.

- **[Safe Resemblance, Silent Propagation: Limits of Text-Similarity Guardrails for AI Agent Safety](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21520)**  `arXiv:2611.21520`  
  _Charles Xiao_
  <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
  When AI agents relay information to one another, an output can preserve an injected instruction's effect on downstream agents even after its wording changes, yet legitimate quotation and template reuse can produce high textual overlap with the source, so text-similarity guardrails that attempt to flag the former risk conflating these two cases. We test this distinction using DonkeyRail, a public classifier combining BLEU, ROUGE-L, and METEOR features, deployed in a controlled three-agent email relay, defining a behavioral measure in which an output counts as functional (the injected procedure induces fresh computations in two subsequent agents) independent of the detector's score, alongside legitimate controls that explicitly authorize quotation and reuse. At its default threshold the classifier misses 8 of 19 functional outputs while flagging 70 of 192 legitimate replies, with every completed quotation and template-reuse reply triggering a false alert; calibrating for low false positives on one template set does not transfer, as the recalibrated threshold detects only one functional output and still produces 15 false alerts on new templates. We also identify an operating-point error in all 21 published classifier configurations, where ROC interpolation replaces direct threshold application, and show formally that high AUC against a surface-level text criterion provides no distribution-free guarantee of high AUC against the behavioral target, while the existing evaluation labels lack the joint observations needed to estimate the gap. An authorization analysis further shows that identical visible transcripts can require different safety decisions depending on the governing policy, a distinction no observation-only classifier can resolve. These results argue for behavioral and policy-grounded evaluation over text-similarity guardrails.
  </details>

- **[Subprocess-Constrained Markov Decision Processes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21471)**  `arXiv:2611.21471`  
  _Jiarui Gan, Debmalya Mandal_
  <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) typically bound expected cumulative cost from an initial state. Such a guarantee can be insufficient when a policy must remain feasible after every history it may encounter. We introduce \emph{subprocess-constrained MDPs} (S-CMDPs), which require the expected discounted cost of each continuation, conditional on any reachable history, to satisfy a bound determined by the current state. This stronger requirement changes the structure and complexity of the problem: we show that stationary policies can be strictly suboptimal, and finding even an approximately optimal stationary policy is NP-hard. We adapt a recently introduced value-set iteration framework to show that history-dependent policies surprisingly overcome both obstacles. Our polynomial-time algorithm represents such a policy implicitly: its expected reward is within $\epsilon$ of the optimal feasible value, it violates each subprocess cost bound by at most $\delta$, and its action distribution can be computed in polynomial time for any given history.

- **[Learning to Reason with Predictive Gains for Generative Recommendation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21441)**  `arXiv:2611.21441`  
  _Zhuoyang Li, Teng Shi, Jiakai Tang, Weijie Yu, Guofu Xie, Xiao Zhang, et al._
  <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Reasoning-enhanced generative recommendation (GR) has attracted increasing attention for leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to improve next-item prediction. However, existing methods primarily optimize reasoning through Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rule-based rewards determined by final recommendation performance, which faces two key limitations: (i) unmeasured predictive contributions within reasoning, where the predictive contributions of individual reasoning steps are not directly reflected in optimization signals, and (ii) weakly discriminative learning signals within groups, where different reasoning trajectories within the group often receive indistinguishable rewards, limiting effective policy optimization. In this paper, we propose GainReason, a simple yet effective training framework that optimizes reasoning through Predictive Gain (PG). We further theoretically establish the alignment between expected PG and the recommendation ranking objective. Building on this signal, we develop PG-based Reasoning Optimization (PGRO) to optimize reasoning generation with sentence-level rewards, supported by a custom causal attention mechanism for efficient PG computation. Additionally, an auxiliary PG-based Reasoning Utilization (PGRU) task adaptively strengthens reasoning-conditioned prediction to complement PGRO. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks demonstrate that GainReason consistently outperforms competitive baselines and achieves substantial performance gains from reasoning.

- **[Benchleaking Is Accelerating: Benchmark contamination has worsened sharply in the past month -- from post-training down to pre-training base models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21315)**  `arXiv:2611.21315`  
  _Yiwei Qin, Muhang Xie, Xuefeng Li, Mohan Jiang, Dayuan Fu, Tiantian Mi, et al._
  <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
  Benchmark contamination is not new, but recent model releases show it in a sharper form. We use the term benchleaking for the case in which a benchmark's own test examples enter training data, so that a score reflects recall rather than capability. This differs from benchmaxxing, which optimizes toward a benchmark without seeing its test set. We document two escalations over the past month. First, in post-training, the traces have become explicit: recent audits show an agent stating hidden test requirements it was never given in 23% of its attempts, where its predecessor did so in none, and continuing to follow them after the task is changed to contradict them (Schaeffer and Räuker, 2026; Lo and Lo, 2026). Second, the problem has reached pre-training. Using few-shot continuation on 20 benchmarks across four public base models, we find that some base models write out other test examples---complete questions, options, and answers---that appear nowhere in their input. For the recently released ERNIE-5.5-Flash-Base, 96% of HumanEval problems leak with their reference implementations and 85% of OpenBookQA questions leak with their answers, against 9% and none for DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base. To quantify what a reported score owes to such examples, we define an Anti-Hack score: the benchmark score recomputed with every leaked example marked wrong and the number of examples unchanged. Averaged over the suite, it costs DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base 1.5 points and Hy3-preview-Base 1.3, against 5.0 for MiMo-V2.5-Base and 16.6 for ERNIE-5.5-Flash-Base. Open base models are the foundation of much downstream research, so benchleaking now affects more than leaderboard rankings. We call for decontamination reports and contamination-adjusted scores to become standard practice.
